    01 · Foundations

    Core concepts

    Open a topic to review its key concepts. Parkinson’s disease is a clinical syndrome with motor and non-motor dimensions that evolve variably over time.

    Key points

    • Parkinson’s disease is characterized clinically by bradykinesia with rest tremor and/or rigidity.
    • Reduced nigrostriatal dopamine alters basal-ganglia-thalamocortical activity.
    • Motor and non-motor features vary across individuals and over time.
    01Definition and pathophysiologyDegeneration, dopamine, and circuit effects

    Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder clinically characterized by bradykinesia with rest tremor and/or rigidity. Neuropathology commonly includes degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta and alpha-synuclein-containing Lewy pathology, although pathology and clinical expression are heterogeneous.

    Reduced nigrostriatal dopamine alters basal-ganglia-thalamocortical activity. Relative underactivity of the direct, movement-facilitating pathway and overactivity of the indirect, movement-suppressing pathway contribute to reduced movement amplitude and speed. Non-dopaminergic systems are also involved, helping explain autonomic, sleep, cognitive, mood, sensory, and other non-motor features.

    02Clinical phenotype and courseHeterogeneity, progression, and phenotype

    Clinical expression varies: tremor-dominant, akinetic-rigid, and mixed patterns are commonly described, but an individual may not fit one stable category. Asymmetry is frequent early in typical PD. Over time, motor fluctuations, postural instability, gait impairment, and non-motor burden may become more prominent.

    Prodromal associations can include hyposmia, constipation, REM sleep behavior disorder, and mood changes. These features are not specific enough to establish PD on their own. Course, function, comorbidities, medication effects, and the person’s priorities all influence clinical assessment and management.

    03Clinical reasoning frameworkSyndrome first, cause second
    1. Establish whether parkinsonism is present, particularly bradykinesia plus rigidity or rest tremor.
    2. Characterize onset, tempo, symmetry, associated features, medication exposure, and functional impact.
    3. Assess whether the pattern is compatible with idiopathic PD or suggests a mimic, secondary cause, or atypical parkinsonian syndrome.
    4. Use investigations selectively to answer a defined clinical question; diagnosis remains primarily clinical.

    02 · Clinical features

    Symptoms and manifestations

    A structured review should cover motor and non-motor domains, timing, severity, fluctuation, and impact on function.

    Key points

    • Bradykinesia includes slowness with decrement in amplitude or speed, or progressive hesitations.
    • Rest tremor is often distal and asymmetric, but is not present in every case.
    • Review autonomic, sleep, cognitive, psychiatric, sensory, pain, and bulbar features alongside motor symptoms.

    Red flags

    Early recurrent falls are atypical for uncomplicated early Parkinson’s disease and warrant broader assessment.

    MMotor featuresBradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, gait, posture

    Bradykinesia

    Slowness is accompanied by decrement in amplitude or speed, or progressive hesitations, during repetitive movement. It may present as reduced spontaneous movement, smaller handwriting, reduced facial expression, hypophonia, difficulty initiating movement, or reduced arm swing. Slowness alone is not sufficient: weakness, pain, apraxia, depression, and other causes should be considered.

    Rigidity

    Increased resistance to passive movement through the range, often relatively uniform and sometimes cogwheel-like when tremor is superimposed. It may affect axial and limb muscles and can contribute to stiffness, reduced rotation, and discomfort.

    Rest tremor

    Often distal, asymmetric, and most apparent when the limb is supported and relaxed. It may lessen with voluntary movement and re-emerge after maintaining a posture. Tremor is not present in every case and can be amplified by stress, attention, or fatigue.

    Gait and postural control

    Possible findings include stooped posture, reduced stride length, shuffling, reduced arm swing, en-bloc turning, start hesitation, freezing (especially at doorways or turns), and impaired postural responses. Early recurrent falls are atypical for uncomplicated early PD and warrant broader assessment.

    NNon-motor featuresAutonomic, sleep, cognitive, mood, sensory
    • Autonomic: constipation, urinary urgency or frequency, erectile dysfunction, sweating changes, drooling, and orthostatic symptoms.
    • Sleep: REM sleep behavior disorder, insomnia, fragmented sleep, restless legs, nocturia, and excessive daytime sleepiness.
    • Cognitive and psychiatric: executive or visuospatial difficulty, slowed processing, depression, anxiety, apathy, hallucinations, and—in some cases—cognitive impairment or dementia.
    • Sensory and pain: hyposmia, pain, paresthesia, and fatigue may occur and can precede or accompany motor features.
    • Bulbar: hypophonia, reduced prosody, dysarthria, drooling, and dysphagia can affect communication, nutrition, and airway safety.

    Ask directly and non-judgmentally: many non-motor symptoms are under-reported, may have multiple causes, and may be more disabling than the visible motor syndrome.

    ↕Fluctuations and dyskinesiaRelate symptoms to medication timing

    With disease progression and repeated levodopa exposure, some individuals develop predictable wearing-off before the next dose, delayed-on or dose failures, and unpredictable OFF periods. Ask about the timing of mobility, stiffness, tremor, dystonia, and non-motor symptoms in relation to each dose, meals, sleep, and activity.

    Levodopa-induced dyskinesia refers to involuntary choreiform or dystonic movements associated with dopaminergic treatment. It may occur near peak drug effect, during transitions, or in other patterns. Distinguish dyskinesia from tremor, akathisia, dystonia, and dyskinesia due to other causes; document phenomenology and timing rather than relying on the label alone.

    03 · Examination

    Clinical history and examination

    A careful, reproducible examination documents the syndrome, asymmetry, severity, function, and features that may point away from typical PD.

    Key points

    • Establish onset, progression, laterality, functional impact, medication exposure, and symptom timing.
    • Test repetitive movements for decrement, compare sides, and distinguish slowness from bradykinesia.
    • Examine eye movements, gait, postural responses, and associated neurological signs.

    Red flags

    Early prominent postural-response impairment and vertical gaze limitation may point away from typical Parkinson’s disease; interpret these alongside the full assessment.

    HHistory-takingOnset, evolution, medicines, and functional impact

    Establish the first symptom, the sequence and rate of progression, laterality at onset, variability during the day, and circumstances that worsen or relieve symptoms. Ask about tremor at rest and during action, slowness, stiffness, gait change, falls, freezing, dexterity, handwriting, speech, swallowing, sleep, mood, cognition, autonomic symptoms, pain, and smell.

    Take a complete prescribed, over-the-counter, and substance history with start dates and dose changes. Specifically consider dopamine-receptor blocking medicines and other agents associated with parkinsonism or tremor. Review vascular risks, head injury, relevant family history, comorbidities, and examination context. Do not infer causation from a medicine list alone.

    Clarify medication response and timing if dopaminergic therapy is already used. A symptom and dose-time record can help characterize fluctuations, but it is not a substitute for direct assessment. Record baseline mobility, activities, cognition, and goals using a consistent clinical framework.

    1General observation and speechObserve before formal motor testing

    Observe spontaneous movement while the person is seated, entering the room, and conversing. Note facial animation, blink rate, posture, spontaneous gestures, turning, voice volume, articulation, prosody, and whether movements are reduced on one side. Allow sufficient time: findings may be subtle and can change with attention or anxiety.

    Listen for hypophonia, monotony, reduced articulation, and palilalia. Assess facial expression and spontaneous blink without assuming that a masked appearance reflects mood. Record whether speech and facial animation change with cueing or effort.

    2Bradykinesia examinationRepetitive movements and decrement

    Demonstrate each movement, then ask for repeated, as-large-and-fast-as-possible movements while the person is seated and supported. Test finger tapping, hand opening and closing, pronation-supination, toe tapping, and heel tapping on both sides. Observe speed, amplitude, rhythm, hesitations, arrests, and progressive decrement; compare sides and repeat if comprehension or effort is uncertain.

    A pattern aligned with parkinsonian bradykinesia is progressive reduction in amplitude or speed, or increasing pauses across repetitions, often with asymmetry. Simple slowness without decrement is less specific. Pain, arthritis, weakness, sensory loss, apraxia, fatigue, and inattention can affect performance and should be documented.

    3Tone and rigidityPassive movement at relaxed joints

    Ask the person to relax. Support the limb and passively move the wrist, elbow, and other joints through flexion-extension and pronation-supination, comparing sides and testing at more than one speed. If resistance is subtle, ask for a contralateral repetitive movement (an activation maneuver) while continuing to assess the relaxed limb.

    Rigidity is a velocity-independent increase in resistance, often present through the range; cogwheeling may be felt when tremor overlays rigidity. Distinguish this from spasticity (typically velocity-dependent with a catch), paratonia, contracture, pain-limited movement, and voluntary resistance. Document distribution and asymmetry.

    4Tremor assessmentRest, posture, action, and re-emergence

    Observe hands fully supported in the lap and feet supported, first at rest and then during conversation or a cognitive distraction task. Look for rhythmic tremor, distribution, frequency impression, and asymmetry. Next assess arms outstretched and in a posture that requires maintaining position; note latency before any re-emergent tremor.

    Assess kinetic tremor during finger-to-nose and functional reach, and observe handwriting or spiral drawing when useful. A classic parkinsonian tremor is often most evident at rest and may re-emerge after a delay during posture; essential tremor is commonly postural or kinetic. These patterns overlap and are not diagnostic in isolation.

    5Gait, turning, and postural responsesGuard safely and describe specific findings

    With appropriate space and guarding, observe rising from a chair, initiation, straight walking, turning, and return. Note base, stride length, speed, cadence, foot clearance, freezing, arm swing, posture, and turning strategy. Ask about freezing triggers; if appropriate and safe, observe a turn or passage through a doorway. Do not provoke a fall to demonstrate a deficit.

    A pull test is performed only by a trained examiner with adequate space and close guarding: explain the maneuver, stand behind, and give a brief, firm backward tug at the shoulders. Observe corrective steps and need for assistance. Abnormal postural responses can occur in PD, but early prominent impairment is a diagnostic warning sign. Omit the test when unsafe and document why.

    6Additional neurological and functional examinationLook for associated signs and alternative localization

    Assess eye movements, especially vertical saccades and gaze range; cerebellar function; pyramidal signs; strength; sensation; coordination; reflexes; and postural alignment. Look for focal weakness, limb apraxia, cortical sensory loss, cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal signs, or supranuclear gaze limitation, which may indicate an alternative or additional disorder.

    Measure orthostatic blood pressure when indicated: rest supine for several minutes, record blood pressure and pulse, then repeat after standing (commonly at 1 and 3 minutes), with assistance and symptom monitoring. A sustained fall of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes is a conventional definition of orthostatic hypotension; interpretation depends on context and local protocol.

    Assess cognition, mood, speech, swallowing, and activities of daily living when clinically relevant. Use validated instruments only with appropriate training, permissions, and interpretation. Consider a supervised ON/OFF assessment when clinically indicated; document medication timing and avoid unsupervised medication withdrawal.

    7Investigations and diagnostic criteriaTarget tests to the diagnostic question

    PD is diagnosed clinically by an appropriately trained specialist using accepted criteria and longitudinal review where needed. The core parkinsonian syndrome requires bradykinesia plus rest tremor or rigidity. A response to dopaminergic therapy may support the assessment but is not, by itself, proof of idiopathic PD.

    Structural MRI is not a confirmatory test for PD; it may help assess alternative causes when the history or examination is atypical. Dopamine-transporter SPECT can help distinguish presynaptic dopaminergic deficit from some non-degenerative tremor disorders when uncertainty remains, but it does not reliably distinguish PD from other degenerative parkinsonian syndromes. Routine laboratory tests are guided by clinical suspicion, not a universal PD panel.

    Examination principle

    Interpret each finding in context. A single sign, scan, or medication response should not replace a complete history, neurological examination, review of medicines, and appropriate specialist judgment.

    04 · Diagnostic reasoning

    Differential diagnosis

    Expand each diagnostic group for distinguishing clues and practical considerations. No single feature is definitive.

    Key points

    • Compare tremor phenomenology, symmetry, tempo, associated features, and medication exposure.
    • Early recurrent falls, severe early autonomic failure, vertical gaze limitation, or cerebellar signs warrant reconsideration.
    • No single feature or levodopa response is definitive; interpret findings in the complete clinical pattern.

    D1Essential tremorAction tremor versus rest-predominant tremor

    Overlap: tremor may impair hand function and can coexist with other movement disorders.

    Clues: essential tremor is typically postural and kinetic, often bilateral, and may involve the head or voice. Parkinsonian tremor is often asymmetric and prominent at rest, with bradykinesia or rigidity.

    Clinical considerations: characterize tremor at rest, posture, and action; examine for decrement, rigidity, gait change, and family history. Tremor phenomenology alone may remain inconclusive.

    D2Drug-induced parkinsonismMedication exposure and temporal association

    Overlap: bradykinesia and rigidity can closely resemble PD; tremor may occur.

    Clues: dopamine-receptor blocking antipsychotics and antiemetics are important causes. Onset may follow initiation or dose escalation, and findings are often more symmetric, though asymmetry does not exclude a drug contribution.

    Clinical considerations: reconcile all medicines and dates, assess for underlying neurodegeneration unmasked by exposure, and coordinate any medication change with the responsible prescriber. Do not stop or alter essential medicines abruptly.

    D3Vascular parkinsonismVascular burden and lower-body gait pattern

    Overlap: gait slowness, short steps, postural instability, and lower-body bradykinesia.

    Clues: cerebrovascular risk, stepwise or temporally linked decline, pyramidal signs, and prominent lower-body involvement may be relevant. Imaging may show vascular disease but findings must match the clinical syndrome.

    Clinical considerations: consider coexisting PD and vascular disease. MRI helps assess structural burden and alternative diagnoses; incidental vascular changes alone do not establish causation.

    D4Progressive supranuclear palsyEarly falls, axial features, and vertical gaze

    Overlap: akinetic-rigid parkinsonism, reduced facial expression, and gait impairment.

    Clues: early unexplained falls, axial rigidity, slowed vertical saccades or supranuclear vertical gaze palsy, dysarthria, and frontal-executive change should raise suspicion.

    Clinical considerations: examine vertical eye movements and postural responses. Levodopa response is often limited, but response varies and should not be used as the sole discriminator.

    D5Multiple system atrophyAutonomic failure with parkinsonism or ataxia

    Overlap: parkinsonism may be the dominant early feature; cerebellar features can also occur.

    Clues: prominent or early autonomic failure, urinary retention, orthostatic hypotension, cerebellar signs, stridor, and rapid progression warrant specialist consideration.

    Clinical considerations: assess autonomic symptoms and signs systematically and investigate alternatives. Autonomic dysfunction is also possible in PD, so timing, severity, and the complete pattern matter.

    D6Dementia with Lewy bodiesCognition, hallucinations, fluctuations, parkinsonism

    Overlap: parkinsonism, cognitive impairment, visual hallucinations, and REM sleep behavior disorder may occur in both disorders.

    Clues: dementia that begins before or around the same time as parkinsonism, marked cognitive fluctuations, recurrent visual hallucinations, and visuospatial impairment are characteristic diagnostic considerations.

    Clinical considerations: apply accepted criteria and document the timeline carefully. Review medicines and avoid assuming that hallucinations or cognitive symptoms have a single cause.

    D7Corticobasal syndromeAsymmetry with cortical features

    Overlap: asymmetric rigidity, bradykinesia, dystonia, and limb dysfunction.

    Clues: limb apraxia, cortical sensory loss, alien-limb phenomena, myoclonus, and language or visuospatial impairment suggest cortical involvement.

    Clinical considerations: the syndrome has multiple possible underlying pathologies. Look for cortical signs rather than attributing marked asymmetry alone to this diagnosis.

    D8Normal-pressure hydrocephalus and other mimicsGait, cognition, urinary symptoms, and structural causes

    Overlap: gait initiation difficulty, short steps, falls, and cognitive change.

    Clues: a broad-based or magnetic gait and urinary urgency with ventriculomegaly may prompt assessment for normal-pressure hydrocephalus. Cervical myelopathy, cerebellar disease, neuropathy, functional movement disorder, and medication effects can also mimic parts of PD.

    Clinical considerations: use examination localization, tempo, associated signs, and appropriately selected imaging or tests. Consider more than one diagnosis when the pattern is mixed.

    Red flags and reassessment

    • Reconsider the diagnosis with rapid progression, early recurrent falls, severe early autonomic failure, early bulbar or respiratory symptoms, vertical gaze limitation, cerebellar signs, pyramidal signs, or prominent cognitive change at onset.
    • Review medications and comorbidity at every reassessment; vascular, musculoskeletal, sensory, cognitive, and psychiatric contributors can coexist with parkinsonism.
    • Interpret treatment response cautiously: inadequate response can reflect dose timing, absorption, adherence, advanced axial symptoms, or an alternative syndrome; apparent response is not uniquely diagnostic of PD.
    • Use investigations to resolve a question, not to replace clinical reasoning. Imaging abnormalities may be incidental, and normal imaging does not confirm PD.
    • Seek specialist input when diagnosis is uncertain, features are atypical, progression is unexpected, or treatment complications are complex. Follow applicable local referral pathways and urgent-care protocols.

    RxLevodopa with carbidopa or benserazideDopamine replacement · most effective motor symptomatic therapy

    Mechanism: levodopa is a dopamine precursor that crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted to dopamine by aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase. Carbidopa and benserazide inhibit this enzyme mainly in peripheral tissues, increasing the fraction of levodopa reaching the brain and reducing peripheral dopamine-related adverse effects.

    Clinical role: improves bradykinesia and rigidity and often tremor. It does not stop neurodegeneration. Formulations and routes differ; response may be affected by gastric emptying, dietary protein competition, and dose timing.

    Clinical considerations: monitor motor benefit, wearing-off, dyskinesia, nausea, orthostatic symptoms, hallucinations, and sleepiness. Long-term use can be associated with motor fluctuations and dyskinesia, reflecting disease progression and changing dopaminergic responsiveness—not addiction. Avoid abrupt withdrawal; follow specialist and product guidance.

    DDopamine receptor agonistsPramipexole · ropinirole · rotigotine · apomorphine

    Mechanism: these agents directly stimulate dopamine receptors, particularly D2-like receptor pathways, without requiring conversion from levodopa. Apomorphine is a potent dopamine agonist used in selected specialist-led settings, including rescue or infusion approaches depending on local practice.

    Clinical role: can reduce motor symptoms and may be used as monotherapy in selected cases or as an adjunct for OFF time. Delivery differs by agent: oral, transdermal, or subcutaneous preparations are used.

    Clinical considerations: nausea, edema, orthostatic hypotension, hallucinations, sudden sleep episodes, and impulse-control disorders are important risks. Screen for gambling, compulsive shopping, hypersexuality, binge eating, and other behavioral change. Risk-benefit assessment is especially important with cognitive impairment, psychosis, daytime somnolence, or falls.

    MAOMonoamine oxidase-B inhibitorsRasagiline · selegiline · safinamide

    Mechanism: MAO-B inhibition reduces central enzymatic breakdown of dopamine, increasing and prolonging dopaminergic activity. Selegiline and rasagiline are generally irreversible inhibitors at therapeutic exposure; safinamide is reversible and also modulates glutamatergic release through additional mechanisms.

    Clinical role: may provide modest symptomatic benefit as monotherapy in selected early disease or reduce OFF time as an adjunct to levodopa, depending on agent and local authorization.

    Clinical considerations: check the full medication list for interactions, including serotonergic and sympathomimetic medicines, and consult current product information. Insomnia may be relevant with selegiline; dyskinesia can increase when combined with levodopa. Do not assume agents in the class are interchangeable.

    COMTCatechol-O-methyltransferase inhibitorsEntacapone · opicapone · tolcapone

    Mechanism: COMT inhibitors reduce levodopa methylation. Entacapone and opicapone act predominantly outside the central nervous system; tolcapone inhibits peripheral and central COMT. When used with levodopa, they prolong levodopa exposure and may extend ON time.

    Clinical role: adjunctive therapy for selected motor fluctuations; they are not used as stand-alone dopamine replacement.

    Clinical considerations: dyskinesia, diarrhea, nausea, hypotension, and urine discoloration can occur. Levodopa-related adverse effects may increase and require review of the overall regimen. Tolcapone carries serious hepatic risk and requires strict adherence to local restrictions and monitoring; verify current product guidance.

    NMDAAmantadineGlutamatergic modulation · dyskinesia

    Mechanism: the full mechanism in PD is not completely established. NMDA-receptor antagonism and modulation of dopaminergic transmission are thought to contribute to its antidyskinetic effect.

    Clinical role: may reduce levodopa-induced dyskinesia and can have modest effects on motor symptoms in selected circumstances.

    Clinical considerations: confusion, hallucinations, ankle edema, livedo reticularis, and sleep disturbance are important considerations. Renal clearance is clinically relevant, so renal function and formulation-specific guidance matter. Abrupt discontinuation after sustained use may cause problems; tapering decisions belong with the prescriber.

    AChAnticholinergic agentsTrihexyphenidyl · benztropine

    Mechanism: central muscarinic receptor antagonism reduces striatal cholinergic activity. This can partially rebalance dopaminergic-cholinergic signaling in basal ganglia circuits and may reduce tremor.

    Clinical role: limited, selective use for tremor-dominant symptoms; these agents have little effect on bradykinesia and are generally avoided when cognitive or autonomic vulnerability is significant.

    Clinical considerations: confusion, memory impairment, hallucinations, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention, and heat intolerance are notable anticholinergic effects. Cognitive burden and falls are particularly important in older adults. Review the total anticholinergic load.

    A2AAdenosine A2A receptor antagonistIstradefylline · adjunctive OFF-time reduction

    Mechanism: adenosine A2A receptors are expressed in striatal neurons of the indirect basal ganglia pathway. Antagonism modulates this pathway and can facilitate movement without directly replacing dopamine.

    Clinical role: an adjunct to levodopa for selected adults experiencing OFF episodes where approved and available.

    Clinical considerations: dyskinesia, insomnia, hallucinations, and other adverse effects can occur. Check current local authorization, interactions, and product information before clinical use.

    06 · Clinical care

    Complications

    Complications are multifactorial. Characterize their time course, severity, contributors, and effect on safety and function.

    Key points

    • Assess falls in relation to gait, orthostatic symptoms, dyskinesia, sensory or visual problems, cognition, medicines, and environment.
    • Ask about swallowing, nutrition, cognition, hallucinations, sleep, mood, pain, and medication timing.
    • Coordinate review across clinical, rehabilitation, communication, nutrition, and safety needs.

    Red flags

    Acute choking or respiratory distress, syncope with injury, new acute confusion, sudden sleep episodes with safety risk, or early recurrent falls require timely assessment under local protocols.

    01Falls, freezing, and mobility declineGait, balance, environment, and medication state

    Falls may relate to freezing, impaired postural reflexes, orthostatic hypotension, dyskinesia, weakness, sensory impairment, visual problems, cognitive dual-task difficulty, sedating medicines, or environmental hazards. Ask about frequency, direction, injury, near-falls, circumstances, footwear, walking aids, and relation to medication timing.

    Examine transfers, turning, gait initiation, stride, foot clearance, dual-task performance when safe, and postural responses with appropriate guarding. Consider physiotherapy, occupational therapy, medication review, vision and cardiovascular assessment, and bone-health or injury evaluation according to context. Early recurrent falls require reassessment for atypical features.

    02Orthostatic hypotension and autonomic dysfunctionSymptoms, measurement, and secondary contributors

    Symptoms can include lightheadedness, blurred vision, weakness, presyncope, syncope, and falls on standing. Assess posture-related symptoms and measure supine-to-standing blood pressure and pulse using a safe, standardized procedure. Review hydration, meals, autonomic dysfunction, and medicines that lower blood pressure.

    Distinguish neurogenic orthostatic hypotension from dehydration, blood loss, cardiac causes, and medication effects. Persistent or severe symptoms, syncope, injury, chest symptoms, or acute deterioration require timely clinical assessment under local protocols.

    03Dysphagia, aspiration, and nutritionSwallowing safety and respiratory consequences

    Look for coughing or choking with meals, prolonged eating, wet voice, drooling, difficulty with tablets, weight loss, dehydration, recurrent chest infection, and silent aspiration risk. Dysphagia may be under-recognized; absence of a volunteered complaint does not exclude it.

    Arrange trained speech and language therapy assessment and instrumental evaluation when indicated. Nutrition and medication administration may need coordinated review. Acute choking, respiratory distress, or suspected aspiration with instability is an emergency.

    04Cognition, hallucinations, and psychosisMedication review and delirium exclusion

    Assess attention, executive and visuospatial function, orientation, fluctuation, sleep, mood, and functional change. Hallucinations may range from presence or passage phenomena to formed visual experiences; determine insight, distress, risk, timing, and relationship to illness or medicines.

    New or acute confusion requires assessment for delirium, infection, metabolic disturbance, sleep disruption, and medication toxicity. Review dopaminergic and other psychoactive medicines with the responsible specialist; abrupt reduction can cause serious deterioration. Use local guidance for management.

    05Sleep and daytime somnolenceREM sleep behavior disorder, insomnia, and sleep attacks

    Ask about dream enactment, injury during sleep, insomnia, nocturia, restless legs, sleep apnea symptoms, fragmented sleep, and excessive daytime sleepiness. Consider collateral history when appropriate and with consent. Distinguish sleepiness related to the disorder from medication effects and other sleep conditions.

    Sudden sleep episodes can create serious safety risks, including while driving or operating machinery. Assess promptly, review medicines, and follow local safety and licensing requirements.

    06Mood, pain, fatigue, and quality of lifeCommon burdens that can be missed in motor review

    Depression, anxiety, apathy, fatigue, and pain may precede motor diagnosis or emerge during disease progression. Pain can be musculoskeletal, dystonic, neuropathic, or central; fatigue may be influenced by sleep, mood, autonomic dysfunction, medication, and comorbidity.

    Ask directly, use appropriate validated tools when trained, and assess for urgent psychiatric risk under local procedures. A motor-focused review alone may underestimate overall disease burden.

    07Motor fluctuations and dyskinesiaPatterns, timing, and functional consequence

    Document OFF periods, delayed or failed ON, early-morning akinesia, predictable wearing-off, unpredictable fluctuations, dystonia, and dyskinesia. Record timing relative to medication, food, activity, and sleep, and determine which periods are most disabling.

    Review adherence, formulation, absorption, and interactions with the specialist. A diary may clarify patterns for clinical review, but medication changes should not be made from a general educational resource.

    08Speech, drooling, and communicationHypophonia, dysarthria, saliva control

    Reduced loudness, monotony, imprecise articulation, and rate changes can affect communication. Drooling may reflect reduced swallowing frequency rather than excess saliva production. Review oral health, swallowing, medication effects, and aspiration risk.

    Speech and language therapy can assess communication and swallowing and provide individualized strategies. Rapid change, new focal neurological signs, or acute swallowing difficulty warrants prompt assessment.

    07 · Multidisciplinary practice

    Roles across the care team

    Coordinated expertise can address motor, non-motor, functional, communication, and safety needs.

    Key points

    • Neurology or movement-disorder expertise supports diagnosis and complex treatment review.
    • Nursing and primary care support longitudinal monitoring, medication reconciliation, and coordination.
    • Physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy, pharmacy, dietetics, and mental health address complementary needs.
    MDNeurology and movement-disorder expertiseDiagnostic formulation and complex treatment review

    Establish and revisit the diagnosis, assess atypical features, coordinate dopaminergic treatment, evaluate fluctuations and adverse effects, and refer for specialist therapies or advanced treatment assessment when appropriate.

    RNNursing and primary careLongitudinal monitoring and coordination

    Support medication reconciliation, monitor general health and comorbidities, recognize changes in mobility or cognition, assess falls and autonomic symptoms, and coordinate follow-up with the multidisciplinary team.

    PTPhysiotherapyGait, balance, transfers, and physical activity

    Assess mobility and falls risk; address gait, freezing, balance, strength, transfers, cueing strategies, and individualized exercise within professional scope and relevant safety guidance.

    OTOccupational therapyActivities, adaptation, and environmental access

    Evaluate functional tasks, fine motor challenges, energy conservation, equipment, and environmental modifications that may support independence and reduce hazards.

    SLTSpeech and language therapyCommunication, voice, and swallowing

    Assess voice, articulation, communication strategies, drooling, and swallowing; recommend further instrumental assessment when indicated and coordinate with nutrition and medical teams.

    +Pharmacy, dietetics, and mental healthMedication safety and whole-person clinical support

    Pharmacists support medication reconciliation and interaction review; dietitians assess nutritional risk, weight change, and medication-food timing considerations; mental health professionals support mood, anxiety, cognition, and behavioral complications.
